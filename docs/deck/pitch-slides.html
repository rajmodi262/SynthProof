SynthProof

Synthetic data that ships with its proof

A differential privacy platform that releases synthetic tabular data together with a signed, machine-verifiable certificate stating both what we can prove and what we can measure — and that keeps a cryptographic ledger of every unit of privacy an organisation has ever spent.

01The Problem

Useful data is locked up — and the usual escape hatch is broken.

87%

Anonymisation fails

Of the US population is uniquely identified by just ZIP + date of birth + sex (Sweeney, 2000). Removing names is not privacy. The Netflix Prize dataset was re-identified from public IMDb ratings (Narayanan & Shmatikov, 2008).

3

Regimes now bite

GDPR Art. 9, HIPAA, and India's DPDP Act 2023 all restrict sharing personal data. The compliance answer is usually "no", so datasets that could train useful models never leave the institution.

0

Evidence shipped

Teams increasingly release synthetic data instead. Almost none of it arrives with any statement of how private it actually is. Generative models memorise — and a synthetic row can be a real person.

"Synthetic" is a description of how the data was produced, not a guarantee about what it reveals. Today that distinction is left to the reader.
02The Gap

Two research communities. Neither ships what a data owner needs.

Camp A — Formal DP

Proves an (ε, δ) bound. Composes. Peer-reviewed.

  • Worst-case. ε is often so loose it says almost nothing — released values of ε = 10 or more are common.
  • Unverified. Nobody checks the implementation matches the proof. A single wrong line silently voids it.
  • Partial. Schema discovery, range clipping, and hyperparameter tuning routinely peek at the data for free.

Camp B — Empirical Auditing

Runs real attacks. Reports real numbers.

  • No guarantee. "This attack failed" is not "no attack succeeds."
  • No budget. Nothing accumulates. Release ten datasets and the leakage compounds silently.
  • No provenance. Results live in a paper appendix, not attached to the artefact that was actually released.

Nobody ships both — bound and measurement, signed, attached to the dataset, and accumulated across every release an organisation makes. That is the gap SynthProof closes.

03Thesis

Every released dataset carries its own evidence.

Empirical canary auditing of tabular DP mechanisms reveals systematic gaps below the formal bound; budget-charged profiling and a signed hash-chain ledger prevent that budget from silently degrading across a multi-release programme.

We prove an upper bound

Every mechanism that touches the sensitive data is charged to an accountant backed by Google's dp_accounting. Nothing is free — not even reading the schema.

We measure a lower bound

Planted canaries and an adversarial attack range recover εaudited as a Clopper-Pearson lower bound against a held-out null. If the auditor cannot detect leakage, it says so with a p-value.

We sign the pair

Both numbers, the mechanism, the seed, and the ledger head are serialised into a Privacy Data Sheet and signed. A third party can verify it without trusting us.

The interesting science lives in the distance between the two bounds. A large gap means our proofs are loose. A small gap means the bound is tight — and that is the number a regulator should actually care about.

05Novelty

Four contributions — three systems, one empirical.

Each ingredient exists somewhere in the literature. None of them ship together, and the composition is where the contribution lives.

01 · Dual-sided assurance

εproved and εaudited computed in the same run, on the same artefact, and reported as a pair.

DP papers report the bound. Auditing papers report attacks. A released dataset gets neither. We make the ratio a first-class, published metric.

02 · Budget-charged profiling

Schema discovery and range estimation are charged to the budget like any other query.

Most pipelines silently read min/max and category domains off the raw data — an unaccounted leak that invalidates the headline ε. We priced it.

03 · Append-only signed ledger

Ed25519 signatures over a SHA-256 hash chain, tracking organisational spend across releases.

DP composes — but only if someone is counting. Nothing in current practice stops a second team re-releasing the same table and doubling the real loss.

04 · Subgroup privacy disparity

Does a uniform ε protect minority records as well as majority ones?

Our strongest empirical question, and the most socially consequential. DP's guarantee is worst-case per record — but measured leakage need not be uniform across groups.

06Architecture

One budget. Every stage charged.

The dashed amber path is the invariant: no stage reads the sensitive table without a charge landing in the accountant, and no certificate is emitted without the ledger head.

08Research Design

Three preregistered hypotheses.

Registered before the sweeps run, with a public commitment to report the result whichever way it falls.

H1 · The gap

εaudited / εproved differs systematically across mechanism families at equal target ε.

Test: AIM vs copula vs independent marginals, ε ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8}, 5 seeds, bootstrapped CIs.

H2 · Disparate impact

Under uniform allocation, minority subgroups exhibit higher measured leakage than majority records.

Test: per-subgroup canary audits on ACS PUMS, stratified by race and sex.

H3 · Smarter allocation

Utility-weighted budget allocation across columns beats uniform at equal total spend.

Test: ledger-driven allocator vs uniform, downstream macro-F1 at fixed ε.

H2 is the one to watch. If measured leakage is not uniform across groups, then a single organisational ε is hiding a fairness problem — and that is a finding worth publishing on its own.
09Roadmap

Four MVPs, each independently demoable.

M0 · Foundations — in progress

A budget you can trust

Accountant on dp_accounting, ε-calibration so target ε is the ε you get, DP domain release, signed ledger, honest baselines. Demo: ask for ε = 2, measure 2.

M1 · Real synthesis

Published mechanisms, real data

AIM via private-pgm, a true copula, UCI Adult and ACS PUMS at 10k+ rows, proper train/test protocol. Demo: a privacy–utility frontier that actually curves.

M2 · The audit engine

Numbers we can defend

Full Steinke one-run audit, LiRA with shadow models, DOMIAS, anonymeter. Demo: deliberately break a mechanism and watch the auditor catch it.

M3 · Ship the proof

Signed, verifiable, reproducible

Persisted keys, standalone verifier, subgroup analysis for H2, allocator for H3, make reproduce. Demo: a stranger verifies our certificate.

The demo that sells it

One screen, end to end:

  • Upload a CSV
  • Set an organisational budget
  • Watch the budget draw down live as profiling and synthesis each take their share
  • See εproved and εaudited plotted as the frontier fills in, with confidence bands
  • Run the attack range against the release
  • Download the signed data sheet
  • Paste it into an independent verifier and get a green tick

That last step is the whole thesis in one interaction: the claim is checkable by someone who does not trust us.

11Applications

Who needs a dataset that argues for itself?

Hospitals & health research

A registry wants to share outcomes data with a university. Today that is an 18-month DPA negotiation. With a data sheet, the ethics board reviews a number, not a promise — and the ledger proves the same cohort was not released five times over.

Banks & fintech

Fraud models need transaction data that never leaves the bank. Synthetic replacements with a certified ε let vendors, hackathons, and model-risk teams work on realistic data with an auditable exposure figure.

Government & statistical agencies

The US Census already ships DP microdata. What it does not ship is an empirical audit alongside the bound. Our data sheet format is a candidate template for any agency releasing under a formal guarantee.

ML teams & MLOps

Training data provenance is becoming a compliance artefact under the EU AI Act. A signed privacy certificate attached to the training set is exactly the evidence a model card should be citing.

Reproducible research

Publish the synthetic companion to your paper's dataset with a verifiable privacy statement, so reviewers can rerun your analysis without an IRB.

Benchmark for the DP community

Our proved-vs-audited sweep is itself a contribution: a public table of how tight each mechanism's bound actually is, on real data, with confidence intervals.

12Risks

What could go wrong, and what we do about it.

Risk · The audit finds nothing

A well-implemented DP mechanism may leak too little for any attack to detect, leaving εaudited ≈ 0 everywhere.

Mitigation: scale canary count until the bound is informative; report the detection floor explicitly; validate the auditor on a deliberately broken mechanism so a null result is credible rather than vacuous.

Risk · We reimplement theory badly

Hand-rolled DP math is exactly how privacy claims become false. We already made this mistake once.

Mitigation: delegate all composition to dp_accounting; differential-test against autodp in CI; never write a bound we cannot cite.

Risk · Scope is too large

Four mechanisms × four attacks × three datasets × five seeds is a lot of compute and a lot of code.

Mitigation: every milestone is independently demoable; if M3 slips, M2 is still a complete and defensible project.

Risk · Utility collapses

At small ε, synthetic data may be useless, making the frontier uninteresting.

Mitigation: that is itself the finding. A frontier showing where usable utility ends is the practical contribution regulators most need.
